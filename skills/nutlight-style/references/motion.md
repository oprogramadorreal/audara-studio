# Nutlight: motion, acting and transitions

How the style moves, on top of code-video's `references/motion.md` (eases, keys, sync, pacing). The author's
own words on motion are in `assets/references/README.md`.

## Explain by doing

- What the voice says, a character or an object does, timed to the words (`ctx.words`). A squirrel takes the
  copy from the tray and carries it to the board. A light runs along a conduit and a drawer lights up. Every
  action has a visible result in the world.
- A demonstration that compares things differs at a glance, and is framed large enough, near the centre, to
  compare. A change too subtle to see, or a comparison small at the frame's edge, came back as notes.
- A funny or emotional beat gets the frame's centre and a moment to land.
- The picture changes with the ideas. One world travelled by the camera can still change place, palette, scale
  and light as the story moves on; a long stretch in one setting looks static.

## Acting

- Characters are always alive: breathing, blinks (seeded per character, so they don't blink together), ear
  twitches, small shifts of gaze, the tail swaying as one rounded form. A frozen character reads as a cut-out.
- A pose change starts and ends at rest, and its parts overlap: the head leads, then the arm. A head turn
  passes through its in-between view, revealing the face's volume.
- Gestures are clear and economical: one point, one touch, one shrug, each meaning something.
- Contact lands on its frame: the paw arrives as the light changes. A prop rides in the paw and is handed to
  the world in the same place and rotation, without a pop.
- Expressions change on beats of the story, not continuously.

## Camera

- A calm, cinematic rhythm: slow pushes and drifts (over 1.2 s) that answer an event and settle, and holds
  while something is read. Portraits get a settled camera; wide shots show the scale of the world.
- Lateral moves part the depth layers (parallax), which is where much of the depth comes from.
- The camera serves what is happening. A pause or a pull back only to show off a set piece read as dead time,
  so judge a composition on screen, in motion, rather than in the plan.

## The setting moves too

Secondary motion belongs to the world: packets travel along conduits, holograms turn slowly, a train crosses
far away, leaves stir, water shimmers. Light stays tied to its source, with restrained pulses on active
screens and machines. Nothing flips at half the frame rate: no strobes.

## Transitions: no hard cuts

Plan every boundary in both scenes: the outgoing scene ends in the state the next one opens from. What
carries it, roughly in order of preference:

- **one camera through one world:** both scenes draw the same place, and the camera travels from one to the
  other;
- **an object that carries over or transforms:** a node of light flies on to become the next scene's star, or a
  drawer opens into a room;
- **a silhouette in the picture wipes across,** a foreground tree or pillar, while both scenes run the same
  camera move;
- **a matched zoom through an opening:** the next scene drawn inside a window, screen or drawer at its scale,
  so inside and outside move as one; or an iris that opens from a lit thing, never a dark disc;
- **a crossfade,** only where nothing better carries it.

Take the opening's position from the object's geometry under the outgoing camera, not from typed screen
pixels, so it stays right when the camera changes. A big change in the music asks for a change in the
picture at the same moment (references/music.md).

## Checks

In clips and stills through each transition and camera move:
- nothing pops in or out during a move, and nothing moves by itself;
- lights stay on their sources as things move; the layers' order stays plausible;
- every reach arrives, and no character freezes or pops between poses;
- each transition carries something from one scene to the next, with no hard cut and no fade to black.
